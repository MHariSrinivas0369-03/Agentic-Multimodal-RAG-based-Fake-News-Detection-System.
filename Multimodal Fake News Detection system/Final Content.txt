CHAPTER 1 – INTRODUCTION
1.1 BACKGROUND

The rapid growth of social media platforms such as X (Twitter), Reddit, and Instagram has fundamentally transformed the way information is created, shared, and consumed. Unlike traditional media, where information dissemination was controlled and verified through editorial processes, modern digital platforms enable instantaneous global communication, allowing millions of users to publish content in real time. While this accessibility enhances information flow, it has also led to the widespread propagation of fake news—misleading or false information presented as factual—which poses serious threats to public trust, societal stability, and decision-making processes.

Historically, fake news detection relied on manual fact-checking performed by journalists, editors, and independent verification organizations. This process involved validating claims, cross-checking sources, and comparing information with trusted references. However, with the exponential increase in user-generated content, manual verification has become impractical due to the scale, speed, and diversity of data.

Early computational approaches focused primarily on text-based fake news detection, utilizing machine learning algorithms such as Logistic Regression, Support Vector Machines (SVM), and Naïve Bayes combined with feature engineering techniques like Bag-of-Words and TF-IDF. While these methods provided baseline performance, they were limited in capturing contextual semantics and complex misinformation patterns.

Subsequent advancements introduced deep learning models, including Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, which improved the modeling of sequential and contextual dependencies in text. More recently, transformer-based models such as BERT, RoBERTa, and Sentence-BERT have significantly enhanced natural language understanding by learning contextual embeddings, enabling state-of-the-art performance in various NLP tasks.

Despite these advancements, a major limitation remains: most models rely heavily on learned patterns from training data and lack the ability to verify factual correctness using external knowledge. As a result, they often fail when encountering unseen or real-world claims that require evidence-based validation. Additionally, modern fake news is inherently multimodal, combining textual narratives with images that may be misleading, manipulated, or taken out of context, further increasing detection complexity.

To address these challenges, recent research has shifted toward multimodal and evidence-based frameworks, which integrate textual understanding, visual consistency analysis, and external knowledge retrieval to improve both accuracy and interpretability.

1.1.1 Evolution of Fake News Detection Techniques

Fake news detection has evolved through multiple stages, beginning with traditional machine learning approaches that relied on handcrafted textual features. These early methods were computationally efficient but limited in capturing deep semantic relationships. The introduction of deep learning enabled models to automatically learn complex patterns from data, improving performance on structured datasets.

The emergence of transformer-based architectures marked a significant breakthrough, allowing models to understand contextual meaning more effectively. However, these models remain largely correlation-based, learning statistical relationships rather than verifying factual correctness. Consequently, their generalization to real-world scenarios remains limited.

1.1.2 Emergence of Multimodal and Evidence-Based Approaches

With the increasing use of images alongside textual content in social media, research has expanded to multimodal fake news detection, where both text and visual information are jointly analyzed. Models such as CLIP enable the measurement of semantic alignment between text and images, helping identify inconsistencies that may indicate misinformation.

Furthermore, retrieval-augmented approaches (RAG) have been introduced to incorporate external knowledge sources such as Wikipedia and fact-checking databases. By retrieving relevant evidence and verifying claims using Natural Language Inference (NLI), these systems move beyond pattern recognition toward fact-based reasoning.

The integration of multimodal analysis and evidence retrieval represents a significant step toward building more reliable and interpretable fake news detection systems.

1.2 MOTIVATION

The motivation for this project arises from the increasing sophistication and impact of fake news in modern digital ecosystems. Unlike traditional misinformation, contemporary fake news often combines misleading textual claims with manipulated or contextually irrelevant images, making it more convincing and difficult to detect.

Existing detection systems face several critical limitations. Many models rely solely on textual analysis and fail to consider visual content, while others focus on multimodal correlations without verifying the factual correctness of claims. Additionally, most deep learning models operate as black-box systems, providing predictions without transparent explanations, which reduces user trust and limits practical applicability.

Another key challenge is the inability of traditional models to handle new and unseen claims. Models trained on labeled datasets often perform well on known data but fail in real-world scenarios where verification requires external evidence. Large language models improve reasoning capabilities but are prone to hallucinations when not grounded in reliable information sources.

This project is motivated by the need to develop a robust, explainable, and evidence-driven fake news detection system that:

Combines textual and visual analysis,
Verifies claims using external knowledge sources,
Provides interpretable outputs with supporting evidence.

Such a system aligns with current research trends in multimodal learning, retrieval-augmented systems, and explainable AI, while also addressing real-world challenges in misinformation detection.

1.3 SCOPE OF THE PROJECT

The scope of this project focuses on the design and implementation of an evidence-guided multimodal fake news detection system capable of analyzing both textual and visual content from social media posts.

Performance may vary for highly ambiguous or emerging claims with limited supporting data.
Computational constraints limit large-scale experimentation on very large datasets.

2.2 RESEARCH GAP

Although significant progress has been made in fake news detection, several critical limitations still exist in current approaches:

1. Most existing models rely heavily on textual information, ignoring the role of visual content, which is often used to mislead users in social media platforms.
2. Multimodal approaches focus mainly on learning correlations between text and images but do not verify factual correctness using external evidence.
3. Existing systems rarely incorporate external knowledge sources such as encyclopedic databases or fact-checking repositories for validating claims.
4. Many deep learning models operate as black-box systems, providing predictions without interpretable reasoning or supporting evidence.
5. Large language model-based approaches improve reasoning capabilities but are prone to hallucinations when not grounded in structured evidence retrieval mechanisms.

These gaps highlight the need for a system that not only detects fake news using multimodal data but also verifies claims through external evidence and provides explainable outputs.

2.3 OBJECTIVES
To develop a multimodal fake news detection system that analyzes both textual and visual data to identify inconsistencies between text and images.
To incorporate an evidence-guided verification mechanism that retrieves supporting information from external knowledge sources and evaluates claim credibility using Natural Language Inference (NLI), while enabling reasoning for new inputs.
To design a feature fusion strategy that integrates textual embeddings, image–text alignment scores, and evidence verification signals into a unified representation for improved classification performance.
To provide interpretable predictions by presenting supporting or contradicting evidence, enabling users to understand the reasoning behind the classification results.

This proposed architecture is a sequential approach to identifying fake news by assessing three main criteria: a textual analysis, a visual evaluation, and an evidential or logical basis for supporting the statement presented. The example of a social media posting containing text content and an image showing similar information is shown in the figure. The text content is passed through a Sentence-BERT encoder to create dense semantic embeddings (contextual meaning is not captured by simple keywords) of the input. Simultaneously, the image is processed with a CLIP encoder, which takes the visual content and projects it into the same semantic space as the text so that the system can determine if the image provides meaningful support of the statement made. Once both the textual and visual stream have been completed, they converge into the RAG (Retrieval-Augumented Generation) module, where the textual embeddings serve as queries to retrieve relevant information from an external knowledge base using FAISS-based vector search. This places factual grounding into the system by introducing actual evidence from the real world, not learned patterns.

After retrieved evidence is evaluated through the calculation of an evidence consistency score to indicate the extent to which the external evidence supports or contradicts the original claim, the evidenced consistency score, the retrieved text embedding of the evidence, and the image-text alignment score will all be used to create a comprehensive integrated representation of the input during the feature fusion stage. The fused features will be inputted to a LightGBM classifier to learn complex decision boundaries associated with the evidence inputs and to produce a final prediction (fake/real) regarding whether the news is fake or real. Finally, the prediction will be processed by an LLM-based explanation layer, which will provide a human-readable justification based on the retrieved evidence. For example, if an original claim states that a public figure made an alleged controversial statement with an unrelated image, the system will detect the inconsistency between the text and the image and will retrieve factual sources based on the claim (that support or refute it), leading to the most likely prediction of "Fake" along with excerpts of evidence provided and with a clearly articulated explanation of why the classification was made in order to enhance the interpretability of the prediction. This end-to-end design provides a multimodal understanding of a news story/claim along with verifiable reasoning beyond the traditional black-box classification model, thus providing the user with confidence in the accuracy and trust of the system for real-world use cases.

The first phase in the pipeline involves using Sentence-BERT (SBERT) for extracting semantic representations from text. This type of model transforms input (news) headlines into dense vector embeddings, capturing contextual meaning instead of the frequency of occurring words.

Working Method:
An input sentence is tokenized, then a transformer-based encoder processes it.
The all-mpnet-base-v2 model generates contextual embeddings.
The output is the fixed-size (768 dimensions) vector representation that captures semantic similarity.

Results of Implementation:
Text Embedding Shape: (20000, 768)
Advantages:
Captures deep semantic meaning of text
Allows for retrieval of similar items based on semantics (in RAG)
Decreases reliance on hand-crafted features

In order to add visual elements into the system, the CLIP model is used to determine how semantically consistent an image is with a piece of text.

Working Method:
Images and text are both encoded in to the same embedding space.
A similarity score is calculated between the image and text representations.
The higher the CLIP score, the stronger the alignment. Conversely, the lower the score, the more mismatch.

Results of Implementation:
CLIP Score Shape: (20000, 1)
Advantages:
Identifies misleading/unrelated pictures
Effectively handles multimodal misinformation
No task-specific training required for this model


A Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) process was created to confirm assertions by utilizing outside sources. This is accomplished via a FAISS system, which performs rapid similarity searches.
How it works:
Only factual news stories were used during the creation of the evidence corpus.
Evidence is indexed in FAISS by using SBERT embeddings.
For any provided input request, the top k matches are located based upon their vector proximity to each other.

Provides factual grounding for predictions.
